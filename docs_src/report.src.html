2Motivation

Who has the problem

Anyone deciding whether to go to an emergency department (ED), sitting in one, or waiting for the bill afterward. There were 155.4 million ED visits in the United States in 2022.1 The question patients ask is simple: "How much am I going to get charged?" Nobody at the bedside can answer it.

As an emergency medicine resident in the Bronx, my patients would often ask me, "How much is this going to cost me?" I would stop in my tracks, a little embarrassed that I didn't know the answer, embarrassed that the medical training system had not taught me, and that I had in a way failed the patient. Because I was at a public hospital, at NYC Health + Hospitals, I would often say, "Don't worry about it. First let's take care of you. Whatever you can't cover, the hospital can take care of," which was a bit of a white lie. Would the hospital actually take care of it?

Fortunately, halfway through my residency, around the start of 2024, state laws were passed in both New York and Connecticut banning medical debt from being reported to credit rating agencies (New York in December 2023, Connecticut in May 2024). Now that I'm working in a wealthier state, in a much wealthier hospital, where the patient population is much more health literate, I haven't been asked the question as much, but it's probably more pertinent.

How they handle it today

Mostly they cannot. The reasons are structural:

  • The price depends on things decided after care. Hospitals bill ED visits at one of five levels, assigned after the visit, plus every test and scan that was ordered.
  • The price depends on the insurer. In Yale New Haven Hospital's own file, a level 4 ED visit has a list price of $1,902, a cash price of $1,122, and negotiated rates that run from $321 to $1,712 across 23 insurers: more than a five-fold spread for the same service.
  • The data is public but unusable. Since 1 January 2021 hospitals must publish their prices.2 Yale New Haven's file is a 122 MB spreadsheet with 724,913 rows, organized by billing code. A patient with a swollen ankle does not know that they need rows 99283 and 73610.
  • The legal right to an estimate skips emergencies. The No Surprises Act gives uninsured and self-pay patients a "good faith estimate", but only for care scheduled at least three business days ahead or on request; CMS guidance says it is not triggered for same-day or walk-in care.3

What that costs

  • Money. For people with job-based insurance, the average price of an ED visit including all services was $2,909 in 2021.4 The ED facility fee alone averaged $713 per visit claim in large-employer plans in 2021, up from $337 in 2012.5 In a 2022 KFF survey, 41% of U.S. adults had debt from medical or dental bills.6
  • Health. In a May 2026 KFF survey, 28% of adults said they had skipped or postponed needed care in the past 12 months because of cost; among uninsured adults under 65 it was 62%.6 Fear of an unknown bill is one reason people stay home with symptoms that need attention.
  • Frustration. The bill arrives weeks later, usually as two or more separate bills, with codes the patient has never seen.

Why it matters

A range with reasons is far better than no number. If a patient knows that an ankle injury will probably cost about $985 under their plan, and could reach $2,791 if a fracture, an MRI and a physical therapy evaluation are all needed, they can decide, plan and ask better questions. And for the patient with chest pressure, the most useful thing a cost tool can say is: go now, the hospital must treat you, and here is what to expect afterward.

What this project does differently

  1. It starts from symptoms, not codes. The patient types a sentence. A clinical agent turns it into the workup an ED would likely order, using 17 scenarios of typical ED workups, which I reviewed and corrected as an ED clinician.
  2. It prices that workup at one real hospital's real rates for the patient's own insurer, line by line, and shows every line.
  3. It gives a range and says what drives it: a lower end, a most-likely figure and a higher end, tied to the visit level and to which "possible" tests get ordered.
  4. It follows the patient past the ED doors. If admission is possible, it prices the inpatient stay and explains observation versus inpatient status, which most patients have never heard of.
  5. It is built so the AI cannot make up a number. Models choose what to price; plain code does every calculation; a check rejects any dollar figure the code did not produce.
  6. It puts safety ahead of cost. Emergency warning signs trigger a "go now" message before anything else happens.

What is not new, honestly

  • The price data is public and other companies already mine it at national scale. This project covers one hospital.
  • Symptom-to-care-setting tools exist (CareRoute above). I did not test them head to head.
  • The clinical scenarios are 17 hand-written templates, not a trained model. Their quality is the quality of the clinician who reviews them.

What each agent does

How they are organized

Why a fixed line and not agents that delegate freely. Each step needs the previous step's output, so there is nothing to negotiate. A fixed order makes the cost of a run predictable, lets the run stop early (the cheapest agent goes first and can end it), and makes a failure easy to locate.

Why the models never do arithmetic. The pricing analyst decides what to price and on which insurer's rates. The tools compute the totals, apply the plan's copay, deductible, coinsurance and out-of-pocket maximum, and store the result. The page reads the numbers from that stored result, never from text a model wrote.

Which model, and why

All four agents use gpt-6-luna, the model the course key allows, at low reasoning effort. One model keeps the setup simple and the cost easy to reason about. Low effort was a measured choice: Section 5 shows that medium effort cost 31% more and produced the same plans and the same prices on all five test cases. The model is set in one place (app/config.py).

The measurements in this report were taken on 22 November 2026 with a personal OpenAI key (OPENAI_API_KEY), because my course Portkey key stopped working that day. The app uses Portkey whenever PORTKEY_API_KEY is present, so the teaching staff's runs go through Portkey.

One real run, step by step

The input: "My dad is 58 and has had chest pressure for the last 30 minutes." Insurance: Aetna. Plan details: $250 ED copay, $1,500 of deductible left, 20% coinsurance, $5,000 left before the out-of-pocket maximum. Run 6b037f5e-5edc, 15.8 seconds.

Step 0: code checks (0 tokens). The text passes the injection and length checks. The keyword screen finds "chest pressure" and the emergency message appears at once, before any AI call:

Step 1: Safety screener. One model call, no tools. It confirms the text is a health problem and that it is potentially life-threatening.

Step 2: Clinical planner. It calls find_scenarios("acute chest pressure"), gets the chest pain scenario as the best match, and reads it with get_scenario. Its plan: visit level 4 to 5; six expected items (ECG, two troponins, blood count, metabolic panel, chest X-ray, blood draw); six possible items (D-dimer, CT angiogram of the chest, BNP, IV medication, sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein); admission possible.

Step 3: Pricing analyst. It calls list_insurers, matches "Aetna", and calls price_ed_visit. The tool returns totals of $1,392, $1,820 and $3,077 and says admission pricing is needed, so the agent calls price_admission, which prices the chest pain inpatient stay (DRG 313) at Aetna's rate of $22,374.

Step 4: Patient advocate. It fetches three guidance notes (emergency care first, observation versus inpatient, what an admission means for your share) and writes the summary. The numbers guard finds every dollar amount in the estimate, so the summary is accepted.

What the patient sees. The headline and three figures are in Figure 1: about $1,764 most likely ($250 copay, the $1,500 deductible, and $14 coinsurance on the $1,820 the insurer allows), from $1,392 to $2,015. Below that, the admission section and the itemized estimate:

6Safety

The largest risk for this app is not technical: a cost number could talk someone out of emergency care. The design starts there.

RiskWhat I builtEvidence
Someone delays emergency care because of costA keyword screen for 13 groups of warning signs (chest pain, stroke signs, trouble breathing, self-harm, overdose, heavy bleeding and others) runs in code before any AI call and shows "call 911 or go now". The screener agent can add flags but can never remove one. The advocate must open with "get care now" when a flag is present. What is still exposed

  • The red-flag screen is keyword-based and in English. An unusual description of a dangerous symptom depends on the screener agent catching it.
  • The rate limit and daily ceiling live in the app's memory, so they reset when the app restarts.
  • The app has had no outside security review.

Who it could help, and how much

  • Patients and families. A number to plan around, the questions to ask, and the knowledge that the hospital must treat them first. With 155.4 million ED visits a year1 and 28% of adults putting off care over cost,6 even a small share of people helped is a large number. I have no measurement yet of how patients would actually use it.
  • The uninsured. For the cut-finger case the app shows the $1,340 cash price next to the $2,271 list price, and tells the patient to ask for the self-pay discount and the financial assistance application. Many patients do not know either exists.
  • The hospital. Billing offices and financial counselors field the same question by phone. A tool that answers it from the hospital's own file, at an AI cost of about one-eighth of a cent, could take pressure off those staff. I have not measured call volumes; this is a hypothesis to test with the hospital.
  • Something newly possible. The admission branch tells a family in the ED that an admission would likely bring them to their out-of-pocket maximum, and that "observation" is billed differently. That conversation rarely happens today.

8Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • Hospital bill only. The emergency physician bills separately, and so may the radiologist. Those bills are not in the totals; the page says so on every estimate.
  • One hospital. Yale New Haven Hospital and its St. Raphael campus.
  • Only nine common ED medicines are itemized (for example acetaminophen, ondansetron, IV antibiotics). Other medications and all supplies are not. The hospital's file lists several packages per medicine at different prices; the app uses the middle-priced one.
  • Traditional Medicare and Medicaid are not in the hospital's file. Those patients see the median insurer rate, which is not their rate.
  • The plan math is simplified to copay, then deductible, then coinsurance, capped at the out-of-pocket maximum. Real plans vary (some waive the ED copay on admission).
  • The predicted workup is a template, not a prediction. 17 scenarios cover common problems. An unusual complaint falls back to a small, conservative plan.
  • Some published values in the file look like placeholders (the "median" and percentile columns are identical for every insurer), so the app uses only the negotiated rate for each insurer.
  • Not validated against real bills. I do not have access to patient bills, and should not.

About the clinical scenarios. The 17 scenarios were drafted with AI help. I reviewed all of them as an ED clinician on 22 November 2026 and changed 15. For example: for headache I moved the head CT, blood work, IV fluids and IV medicine from "possible" to "expected" and removed the lumbar puncture; for fainting I made admission likely and the troponin expected; for back pain I removed the imaging and added acetaminophen and a lidocaine patch; for an allergic reaction I made the three IV medicines expected. The current set is in SCENARIOS_REVIEW.md. One clinician's review is a start, not a validation.

What I would build with another month

  1. The physician's bill. Add ED professional fees from the Medicare physician fee schedule, so the estimate covers both bills.
  2. Validation. Run published real cases (for example KFF Health News "Bill of the Month" ED bills) through the app and report how far off it is.
  3. Review of every scenario by more than one ED clinician, with a record of who signed off.
  4. "Check my bill." When the bill arrives, compare it line by line with the estimate and flag what was not expected.
  5. More hospitals. Every hospital publishes the same file format, so the extraction script can be pointed at the next one.
  6. A pilot with a hospital financial counseling team, to measure whether estimates change what patients ask and how long calls take.
  7. Spanish, including the red-flag screen.
